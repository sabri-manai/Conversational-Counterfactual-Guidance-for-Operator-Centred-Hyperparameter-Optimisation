\title{Conversational Counterfactual Guidance for Operator-Centred
Hyperparameter Optimisation in Industrial Anomaly Detection}

\begin{abstract}
Hyperparameter optimisation is essential for high-performing
machine-learning systems, yet its logs are rarely reused for
interpretation. In industrial visual anomaly detection, configuration
must balance detection quality against training-data availability,
review effort, and data quality. Optimisation tools expose
scores or rankings, but rarely show how a configuration should change
to satisfy operator needs. We present
Counterfactual Explainable Hyperparameter Optimisation (CF-XHPO), which
reuses trials to train a surrogate and generate constrained
what-if recommendations through a schema-constrained conversational
interface. We separate surrogate-level query satisfaction from
main-model confirmation by retraining PatchCore for 24 recommendations
each on the MVTec Anomaly Detection benchmark and the Proditec
production dataset. All 48 satisfy their surrogate queries. On
Proditec, 21/24 meet the target after retraining, with mean confirmed F1
score of 0.815, mean surrogate-to-model gap of $-0.024$, and test area
under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.947.
MVTec exposes a reliability boundary: despite a grouped cross-validated
coefficient of determination ($R^2 = 0.995$), none of the 24 reaches the
target. The discrepancy occurs on rerun validation data ($-0.204$),
while validation-to-test transfer is small ($+0.013$); models retain
per-category AUROC of 0.827 although pooled AUROC falls to 0.497. These
findings motivate query-region evaluation, category-specific
calibration, and main-model confirmation. On these logs,
constrained retrieval matches off-log generation, supporting a
retrieve-first policy when coverage is adequate. A 22-participant study finds high task validity in both workflows, with conversational participants reporting greater ease of option comparison. Parser evaluation identifies deterministic handling of relative constraints as a key requirement.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
% =======================================================================

Industrial visual inspection increasingly relies on machine-learning
models to detect defects, reduce manual inspection effort, and support
more consistent quality-control decisions. Deep learning has driven
rapid progress in this area \cite{xie_deep_2024}, with benchmarks and
methods such as the MVTec Anomaly Detection (MVTec~AD) benchmark,
PatchCore, and EfficientAD among the most
widely used reference points
\cite{bergmann_mvtec_2019,roth_towards_2022,batzner_efficientad_2024}.
In this setting, however, a useful model is not defined by detection
performance alone. Engineers and operators must also consider practical
constraints such as the amount of training data available, the expected
review effort, the quality of the input data, and the cost of
retraining or deploying a new configuration. Hyperparameter
optimisation (HPO) is therefore not only a technical search problem,
but also a decision-support problem in which different configurations
represent different operational trade-offs.

Automated HPO frameworks are effective at exploring large configuration
spaces and identifying high-performing trials, as illustrated by tools
such as Optuna and broader work on HPO benchmarking and multi-fidelity
evaluation
\cite{10.1145/3292500.3330701,eggensperger_hpobench_2021,pfisterer_yahpo_2021}.
Yet, their outputs are usually presented as ranked configurations,
optimisation traces, visual analytics views, or parameter-importance
summaries \cite{NEURIPS2021_12ced2db,zoller_xautoml_2023,mu_shrinkhpo_2024}.
These outputs help users understand which configurations performed
well, but they provide limited support for actionable what-if
questions: what should change if the operator wants similar quality
with less review effort, a lower training fraction, or better
robustness to data corruption? This limitation is particularly relevant
in industrial contexts, where users often reason in terms of
constraints and preferences rather than low-level model
hyperparameters.

This paper addresses this problem by proposing Counterfactual Explainable
Hyperparameter Optimisation (CF-XHPO), a framework that turns HPO logs
into an interactive recommendation layer. Instead of discarding
optimisation trials once the best configuration is found, CF-XHPO uses
the log to train a surrogate of the configuration--performance
relationship and to retrieve or generate alternatives under new
operator constraints. The surrogate provides a millisecond-scale
screening layer; main-model confirmation remains the final evidence for
a selected configuration. On top of this layer, the framework compares
complementary counterfactual-generation methods with constrained log
retrieval, building on work on diverse, feasible, and actionable
counterfactual explanations
\cite{10.1145/3351095.3372850,dandl_multiobjective_2020,poyiadzi_face_2020,DEOLIVEIRA2023}.
These alternatives respect a separation between hard model
hyperparameters and soft operator-facing controls, so recommendations
stay technically valid while remaining expressed in terms operators can
act on. To support such interaction, CF-XHPO includes a schema-constrained
conversational layer, motivated by recent work on natural-language
model explanation and conversational explainable artificial
intelligence (XAI)
\cite{slack_explaining_2023,liao_questioning_2020,he_is_2025}, through
which operators can state goals in plain language, compare ranked
alternatives, and receive concise explanations of the main
trade-offs.

We evaluate the framework on the MVTec visual anomaly-detection
benchmark and the Proditec industrial inspection use case. Both use the
same PatchCore pipeline and configuration space, and every primary
recommendation is retrained with the underlying model. This design lets
us distinguish whether a recommendation satisfies its structured query
under the surrogate from whether its predicted quality survives
main-model confirmation. We further examine the reliability of the
query-selected region, compare off-log generation with constrained
retrieval, measure complete-interaction cost, and conduct a user study
of the conversational workflow.

Manai et al.\ \cite{manai_cfhpo_conference} demonstrated
soft-control-constrained counterfactual guidance on tabular
classification benchmarks. The present paper extends that work,
retaining its hard/soft control separation and log-reuse idea and
rebuilding the remainder for industrial visual anomaly detection. Its
main contributions are a
two-dataset industrial evaluation with main-model confirmation of 60
configurations and score-level diagnosis of the 48 primary
recommendations; evidence that global surrogate fidelity does not
guarantee reliability in query-selected regions, including a
selection-conditioned gap and pooled category score-scale failure; and
a schema-constrained conversational layer with workflow evaluation
covering factual-anchor sensitivity, constrained retrieval,
multi-alternative runtime, a labelled parser test, and a
22-participant study
(Sections~\ref{subsec:gap-diagnosis}--\ref{sec:user-study}).
Section~\ref{subsec:rw-conference} sets out the comparison in full.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}
% =======================================================================

The proposed framework sits at the intersection of four research
threads: explainable HPO, counterfactual explanations, conversational
XAI, and industrial anomaly detection. This section reviews each
thread and explains how they combine in our work.

\subsection{Explainable and Interactive Hyperparameter Optimisation}
\label{subsec:rw-xhpo}

Hyperparameter optimisation (HPO) is central to modern
machine-learning workflows. Frameworks such as Optuna provide flexible
black-box search over large spaces while recording trial-level results
\cite{10.1145/3292500.3330701}, and surveys cover strategies from grid
and random search to Bayesian, evolutionary, and learning-based
approaches \cite{a_ilemobayo_hyperparameter_2024,pr11020349}. Their
outputs, however, are optimisation traces, best-trial summaries, or
ranked configurations: dashboards and visual-analytics tools such as
XAutoML \cite{zoller_xautoml_2023} show \emph{which} configurations
performed well, not \emph{how} a configuration should change to meet a
new operator constraint, and surrogate-based importance analysis
\cite{theodorakopoulos_hyperparameter_2024} serves benchmarking rather
than user-facing recommendation.

A parallel effort makes HPO and automated machine learning (AutoML)
more transparent: partial
dependence plots explain hyperparameter effects across the explored
space \cite{NEURIPS2021_12ced2db}, ShrinkHPO exposes and prunes less
promising search regions \cite{mu_shrinkhpo_2024}, explainable Bayesian
optimisation targets users who need to understand the search process
itself \cite{10.1007/978-3-032-08324-1_3}, and related work argues for
opening optimisation procedures to inspection and human interpretation
\cite{yates_explainable_2025,van_stein_explainable_2025}.

Together, these works establish that transparency, interaction, and
surrogate modelling matter in HPO and AutoML, but they mainly support
optimisation analysis, benchmarking, or visual inspection rather than
post-hoc configuration guidance under user-defined practical
constraints.

Green artificial intelligence (Green AI) treats efficiency as an
evaluation criterion and tracks model-development energy and carbon
costs \cite{strubell2019energy,schwartz2020green,
henderson2020towards,patterson2021carbon}. Because HPO multiplies
training costs, we report component and complete-interaction latency for
log reuse; energy or carbon claims require power telemetry.

\subsection{Counterfactual Explanations for Configuration Guidance}
\label{subsec:rw-cf}

Counterfactual explanations describe how an input would need to change
to obtain a desired model output \cite{wachter_counterfactual_2017}.
They are widely studied in explainable artificial intelligence because they are
contrastive, actionable, and often easier for users to interpret than
global feature-importance summaries \cite{verma_counterfactual_2024}.
Diverse Counterfactual Explanations (DiCE) introduced a model-agnostic
framework for generating diverse counterfactual explanations,
emphasising multiple plausible alternatives rather than a single
nearest change \cite{10.1145/3351095.3372850}, and multi-objective
methods show that validity, proximity, sparsity, and diversity can be
treated as competing objectives \cite{dandl_multiobjective_2020}.
Broader reviews organise this literature around validity, proximity,
sparsity, diversity, feasibility, and actionability
\cite{verma_counterfactual_2024}, distinguishing counterfactual
explanations as target states from recourse as feasible interventions
\cite{karimi_algorithmic_2021} and requiring that proposed changes be
controllable and realistic for the user \cite{ustun_actionable_2019}.

Feasibility has also been studied through data-supported counterfactual
paths: Feasible and Actionable Counterfactual Explanations (FACE)
argues that nearest counterfactuals may be unrealistic
outside plausible regions of the data distribution and constructs
paths supported by observed data \cite{poyiadzi_face_2020}, while CFNOW
uses tabu search for model-agnostic counterfactual generation on
tabular, image, and text data \cite{DEOLIVEIRA2023}. These approaches
motivate treating retrieval from observed configurations and generation
beyond them as empirical alternatives rather than assuming that
off-data generation is always necessary. Spreitzer et al.\ show that
formally valid counterfactuals are not
automatically perceived as practical by users
\cite{spreitzer_evaluating_2022}, an observation directly relevant to
operator-facing HPO recommendations.

Counterfactuals have also been used to steer and personalise models
interactively
\cite{chandramouli_interactive_2023,bhattacharya_exmos_2024,misitano_towards_2022}.

\subsection{Conversational Interfaces for Human-Centred Machine-Learning Workflows}
\label{subsec:rw-llm-human}

Conversational interfaces make machine-learning systems more
accessible: TalkToModel shows natural-language dialogue helps users
query and understand explanations without manually selecting tools
\cite{slack_explaining_2023}, and recent work asks whether
conversational XAI assistants improve understanding, trust, and
reliance over dashboards \cite{he_is_2025}. Both lower interaction
barriers and warn that fluent explanations may increase overreliance
where uncertainty is not communicated.

Design-oriented XAI research frames explanation needs as user
questions that interfaces should support \cite{liao_questioning_2020},
and human-centred perspectives on explainability in the era of large
language models emphasise
users, contexts, and downstream effects over black-box opening
\cite{ehsan_human-centered_2024,reinhard_effects_2026}. This
perspective informs our user study, which evaluates the interface
inside a decision-support workflow rather than in isolation.

In AutoML, human-centred studies show that users need support for
understanding automation and deciding when to trust automated results
\cite{drozdal2020trust,xin_whither_2021,wang_autods_2021}, and
interactive systems expose pipelines, optimisation histories, and
trade-offs through inspectable interfaces
\cite{zoller_xautoml_2023,weidele_autodoviz_2023,franke_autorl_2024,10.1007/978-3-031-48232-8_42}. Our
framework uses a schema-constrained conversational layer: the language
model maps requests into validated objectives and constraints, while
recommendation remains with the surrogate and counterfactual
pipeline.

\subsection{Industrial Visual Anomaly Detection as an Explanation-Critical Setting}
\label{subsec:rw-ad}

Industrial visual anomaly detection aims to identify defective or
unusual regions in product images, often under limited supervision.
Benchmark datasets such as MVTec~AD have become standard for
evaluating unsupervised and semi-supervised anomaly-detection methods
in industrial inspection scenarios \cite{bergmann_mvtec_2019}.
Representative approaches span self-supervised, distribution-modelling,
embedding-based, and deployment-oriented designs
\cite{li_cutpaste_2021,defard_padim_2021,roth_towards_2022,batzner_efficientad_2024}.

Although anomaly-detection methods have become increasingly strong,
deployment still depends on configuration choices such as backbone,
image size, feature layers, coreset sampling, nearest-neighbour
settings, and aggregation strategy. These technical choices interact
with practical constraints such as available training data, review
effort, latency, and tolerance to corrupted or noisy data. Standard benchmarks focus on detection metrics
\cite{bergmann_mvtec_2019,roth_towards_2022}, leaving less support for
choosing among configurations with similar quality but different
operational costs, as the Proditec production use case
exemplifies.

\subsection{Positioning of This Work}
\label{subsec:rw-positioning}

The reviewed threads provide the building blocks for this paper: HPO
and AutoML automate configuration search, explainable HPO makes it
inspectable, counterfactual explanations provide actionable
alternatives, conversational XAI improves accessibility, and
industrial anomaly detection motivates the deployment setting. Our
work combines them by using HPO logs as the basis for constrained
configuration recommendation exposed through a schema-constrained
conversational interface. The evaluation separates three questions
that are often conflated: whether a recommendation satisfies its query
under the surrogate, whether the underlying model confirms the target,
and whether users can act on the resulting alternatives. It also tests
when retrieval from the existing log is sufficient and when generation
beyond it adds measurable value.

\subsection{Relation to the Conference Version}
\label{subsec:rw-conference}

Manai et al.\ \cite{manai_cfhpo_conference} introduced a preliminary
version of this framework. Two ideas carry over from that work: the
separation of hard model hyperparameters from soft, human-controllable
parameters, and the reuse of a completed optimisation log as the basis
for counterfactual configuration queries answered through a surrogate.
The remainder has been rebuilt.

Their study was conducted on four tabular binary-classification
benchmarks with XGBoost as the main model. Soft parameters there
described data-handling actions such as the training-row fraction,
injected missingness, resampling strategy, feature fraction, and the
labelled ratio of a self-training scheme. An operator fixed those
values manually, and DiCE and CFNOW then varied only the model
hyperparameters around a single factual seed. Candidates were
re-evaluated by retraining XGBoost over five seeds, and the reported
evidence was an aggregate per-dataset comparison of surrogate,
generated, and retrained F1, together with one case study showing a
moderate surrogate-to-model correlation (Pearson $r = 0.53$ over 16
candidates). That paper contained no structured query schema, no
natural-language interface, no evaluation with human participants, and
no industrial use case; a language-model summarisation layer and the
Proditec deployment were named only as future directions.

The present paper moves the framework to industrial visual anomaly
detection with PatchCore, replaces manually fixed soft parameters with
a validated query schema over deployment-facing controls, adds
constrained log retrieval as a first-class alternative to generation,
and makes main-model confirmation the reporting standard rather than an
aggregate check. It also implements and evaluates the conversational
layer that Manai et al.\ proposed, and evaluates the resulting
workflow with human participants. Three supporting analyses are also
new: a thirteen-model surrogate benchmark with grouped cross-validation
and duplicate control (Section~\ref{subsec:surrogate-fidelity}), an
anchor-sensitivity sweep over five factual configurations
(Section~\ref{subsec:multi-factual}), and component and
complete-interaction cost accounting
(Section~\ref{subsec:compute-cost}).
Table~\ref{tab:conference-delta} summarises the difference. The new
material comprises the whole of Sections~\ref{sec:evaluation}
and~\ref{sec:user-study}, the query schema and interface of
Sections~\ref{subsec:cf-generation} and~\ref{subsec:interface}, and the
problem formulation of Section~\ref{subsec:problem-formulation}.

\section{The CF-XHPO Framework}
\label{sec:method}
% =======================================================================

This section presents the proposed framework for generating
operator-centred counterfactual guidance from hyperparameter
optimisation logs. The main idea is to transform optimisation trials
into a reusable configuration--performance dataset, train a surrogate
model over this dataset, and use the surrogate as a fast what-if layer
for counterfactual recommendation. A schema-constrained conversational
interface then allows users to express objectives and constraints in
natural language, while the recommendation process remains grounded in
validated structured queries, feature domains, and the
candidate-production methods.

\subsection{Configuration Guidance Problem}
\label{subsec:problem-formulation}

We consider an industrial visual anomaly-detection pipeline whose
behaviour is determined by a configuration vector decomposed into two
parts:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x} = (\mathbf{h}, \mathbf{s}),
    \label{eq:configuration}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{h}$ denotes hard model hyperparameters, the technical
choices internal to the anomaly-detection model, and $\mathbf{s}$
denotes soft operator-facing controls, the operationally meaningful
choices an operator reasons about: the fraction of training data used,
the review budget, and the assumed corruption level of the training
data. The concrete variables instantiating $\mathbf{h}$ and
$\mathbf{s}$ in our implementation are listed in
Section~\ref{subsec:logs-features} (Table~\ref{tab:configuration-space}).

The anomaly-detection model produces a continuous anomaly score $a_i$
for each validation image $i$, rather than a binary decision. The
review budget $\beta \in (0,1]$ defines the fraction of highest-scoring
images an operator inspects; this is a common deployment pattern in
industrial inspection, where a human makes the final call on the images
the model flags as most likely anomalous. Given $n$ validation images,
the number of reviewed images is:
\begin{equation}
    m_{\beta} = \lceil \beta n \rceil.
    \label{eq:review-budget-count}
\end{equation}
The review threshold $\tau_{\beta}$ is the score of the
$m_{\beta}$-th highest anomaly score. Each validation image also carries
a ground-truth label $y^{\mathrm{val}}_i \in \{0,1\}$ indicating whether
it is truly anomalous. The predicted anomaly label is:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{y}^{\mathrm{val}}_{i,\beta} =
    \begin{cases}
        1, & a_i \geq \tau_{\beta}, \\
        0, & a_i < \tau_{\beta}.
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:review-budget-prediction}
\end{equation}
The optimisation target for a configuration $\mathbf{x}$ is then:
\begin{equation}
    v(\mathbf{x}) = F_1\!\left(\mathbf{y}^{\mathrm{val}},
                               \hat{\mathbf{y}}^{\mathrm{val}}_{\beta}\right).
    \label{eq:hpo-target}
\end{equation}

The soft controls act at different points of the pipeline: training
fraction sets how much normal data fits the model, corruption level
sets the data-quality condition, and review budget sets the
thresholding rule, an operator-facing deployment trade-off between
detection quality and inspection effort rather than a model
hyperparameter in the narrow sense.

The objective is not to find a high-performing configuration in
isolation but to support guidance after optimisation has finished. The
factual configuration $\mathbf{x}_0$, the operator's current
starting point, is always instantiated as a logged trial: resolved
automatically by the interface (Section~\ref{subsec:interface}) and
fixed to specified trials for reproducibility in the technical
evaluation (Section~\ref{subsec:protocol}).
Given the factual configuration $\mathbf{x}_0$, its surrogate-predicted
quality $\hat{f}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_0)$, and a user request
specifying a desired objective and soft constraints, the task is to
return a set of alternative configurations:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{C} = \{\mathbf{x}'_1, \ldots, \mathbf{x}'_K\},
    \label{eq:cf-set}
\end{equation}
where $K$ is the requested number of alternatives. This turns
hyperparameter configuration into a constrained counterfactual
recommendation problem.

\subsection{Framework Overview}
\label{subsec:framework-overview}

Figure~\ref{fig:framework-overview} illustrates the proposed
framework. The process starts with an automated HPO run in which
Optuna's tree-structured Parzen estimator (TPE) samples both hard
hyperparameters and soft operator-facing controls. Each completed trial stores a configuration and its
resulting validation F1 score, producing a trial-level
configuration--performance log. This log is cleaned, typed, and
transformed into a surrogate-ready dataset.

A surrogate model is trained to approximate the mapping from
configurations to validation performance. The surrogate provides a
reusable approximation layer that allows the system to explore local
what-if alternatives without retraining PatchCore \cite{roth_towards_2022}
for every user query. The candidate-production methods then search
this surrogate space for configurations that satisfy a desired
performance objective and selected soft constraints.

The final layer is the conversational query interface: natural-language
goals are mapped to a structured query schema, validated, repaired, and
snapped to the allowed domains before generation, and the output is a
ranked list of configurations with predicted quality, soft-control
values, constraint status, and textual trade-off explanations.

\subsection{Optimisation Logs and Configuration Features}
\label{subsec:logs-features}

Each completed HPO trial produces a record:
\begin{equation}
    (\mathbf{x}_i, v_i) = ((\mathbf{h}_i, \mathbf{s}_i), v_i),
    \label{eq:trial-record}
\end{equation}
where $v_i = v(\mathbf{x}_i)$ is the validation F1 score obtained by
training and evaluating the pipeline under that configuration. Given
$N$ completed trials, the complete optimisation log is:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{log}} = \{(\mathbf{x}_i, v_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}.
    \label{eq:log-dataset}
\end{equation}

In our implementation, hard hyperparameters define the PatchCore-style
pipeline: backbone, batch size, centre crop, coreset ratio, image size,
feature layers, maximum patches per image, nearest neighbours,
pretrained weights, and anomaly-score reduction; the soft controls are
defined in Section~\ref{subsec:problem-formulation}. We deterministically
cast categorical features, snap continuous controls, discard invalid or
incomplete trials, and retain only features used by the surrogate and
candidate-production methods. The cleaned MVTec and Proditec logs
contain $9{,}976$ and $2{,}999$ trials, respectively.

Table~\ref{tab:configuration-space} summarises the configuration
variables used by the framework. The same configuration space applies
to both the MVTec and Proditec use cases, enabling direct comparison
of surrogate fidelity and counterfactual quality across the two
datasets.

\subsection{Surrogate-Based Performance Modelling}
\label{subsec:surrogate}

Training the main anomaly-detection model for every possible user
query would be computationally expensive. The framework therefore
trains a surrogate model:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{f}_{\theta}: \mathcal{X} \rightarrow [0,1],
    \label{eq:surrogate}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{X}$ is the mixed categorical--numerical configuration
space and $\hat{f}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})$ is the predicted validation
F1 score. The surrogate is trained on a training split
$\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}} \subseteq \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{log}}$ (the
exact split used in our experiments is given in
Section~\ref{subsec:protocol}) by minimising the squared prediction
error:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}(\theta)
    =
    \frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}|}
    \sum_{(\mathbf{x}_i,v_i)\in \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}}
    \left(v_i - \hat{f}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_i)\right)^2.
    \label{eq:surrogate-loss}
\end{equation}

The trained surrogate serves two roles: fast approximation of model
quality for unseen or modified configurations, and local what-if
exploration that lets candidate-production methods search for
configurations meeting a target without retraining PatchCore.
Generated candidates need not coincide with any logged trial, and for
such configurations the surrogate is the only source of estimates;
whether generation beyond the log is \emph{needed} is an empirical
question of log coverage, tested directly in
Section~\ref{subsec:retrieval-comparison}.
Crucially,
the surrogate is used as an interactive search layer, not as final
evidence that a configuration will necessarily perform identically when
retrained. Selected recommendations are therefore subject to main-model
confirmation in the technical evaluation.

\subsection{Counterfactual Configuration Generation}
\label{subsec:cf-generation}

Given a factual configuration $\mathbf{x}_0$ and a user query, the
counterfactual generation layer searches for alternative configurations
$\mathbf{x}'$ that meet a desired performance objective while
respecting user-defined constraints. The user objective is converted
into a desired prediction interval $R(q, \mathbf{x}_0) = [L, U]$,
where $q$ denotes the structured query. The framework supports three
objective types:
\begin{equation}
R(q,\mathbf{x}_0)=
\begin{cases}
[v, 1], & \text{if } q_{\mathrm{obj}}=\texttt{target\_min}(v), \\
[\hat{f}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_0)+\delta, 1],
        & \text{if } q_{\mathrm{obj}}=\texttt{delta\_improve}(\delta), \\
[L, U], & \text{if } q_{\mathrm{obj}}=\texttt{target\_band}(L,U).
\end{cases}
\label{eq:objective-types}
\end{equation}

Soft constraints restrict the candidate space for operator-facing
controls. For numerical controls such as training fraction and review
budget, a constraint can be free, fixed to a value, or restricted to a
range. For the categorical corruption level, it can be free, fixed, or
restricted to an allowed set. These constraints define a feasible
region:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{X}_{q} = \{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{X} :
    \mathbf{s} \text{ satisfies the soft constraints in } q\}.
    \label{eq:query-feasible-region}
\end{equation}

A candidate satisfies its recorded query under the surrogate when:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{f}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}') \in R(q,\mathbf{x}_0),
    \qquad
    \mathbf{x}' \in \mathcal{X}_{q}.
    \label{eq:cf-validity}
\end{equation}
We call the proportion of candidates satisfying
Eq.~\ref{eq:cf-validity} the \emph{surrogate-level query satisfaction
rate}. It answers whether candidate production and constraint handling
return what was requested according to the model used for search. The
\emph{confirmation rate} instead measures the proportion whose
retrained PatchCore score meets the same objective
(Section~\ref{subsec:cf-validity}). Reporting both prevents
surrogate-level compliance from being mistaken for evidence about the
underlying model. When candidate production does not return enough
query-satisfying alternatives, the system retains near-compliant
candidates but marks unmet constraints explicitly and ranks them below
fully compliant alternatives.

Candidate production compares two generators with one retrieval
method. DiCE \cite{10.1145/3351095.3372850} produces diverse
alternatives, while Actionable Counterfactual Explanations (ACFX)
\cite{bobek_acfx} uses explainable boosting
machines to seek alternatives close to the factual configuration.
\textsc{LogTopK} filters observed HPO trials by the query constraints
and re-ranks them using the surrogate. It therefore cannot recommend
configurations outside the log. The archived notebooks use the legacy
identifier \texttt{CFNOW} for this procedure, but it is not the CFNOW
tabu-search algorithm \cite{DEOLIVEIRA2023}. The comparison tests
whether generation beyond the log adds value while holding surrogate
scoring fixed. Each method receives the factual configuration, the
surrogate model, the desired performance interval, the permitted
feature ranges, and the required number of alternatives:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{C}_{m} =
    G_{m}(\mathbf{x}_0, \hat{f}_{\theta}, R, \mathcal{X}_{q}, K),
    \label{eq:generator}
\end{equation}
where $G_m$ denotes the procedure used by method $m$.

Candidates are post-processed to remove invalid rows, enforce feature
domains, and rank alternatives. Ranking prioritises objective
satisfaction, soft-constraint satisfaction, predicted quality, and
proximity to the factual configuration. Proximity is measured by a
mixed-type distance:
\begin{equation}
    d(\mathbf{x}',\mathbf{x}_0)
    =
    \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}}
    \frac{|x'_j - x_{0j}|}{r_j}
    +
    \sum_{j \in \mathcal{C}}
    \mathbb{I}[x'_j \neq x_{0j}],
    \label{eq:mixed-distance}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{N}$ and $\mathcal{C}$ are the numerical and categorical
feature sets, and $r_j$ is the range of numerical feature $j$.

\subsection{Conversational Query Interface}
\label{subsec:interface}

The conversational interface allows users to express configuration
goals in natural language while keeping the recommendation process
constrained and reproducible. A user request $u$ is mapped to a
structured query $q = \Phi(u)$, where $\Phi$ denotes the
language-model-based parser followed by deterministic validation,
repair, normalisation, and snapping. The parser is implemented with an
instruction-tuned open-weight language model (Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct with
Phi-3-mini and Llama-3.2-3B supported as alternatives).

The structured query has three components. The \emph{objective} is
\texttt{target\_min}($v$), \texttt{delta\_improve}($\delta$), or
\texttt{target\_band}($L,U$). The \emph{soft constraints} give
training fraction and review budget as \texttt{free},
\texttt{fixed}, or \texttt{range}, and corruption level as
\texttt{free}, \texttt{fixed}, or \texttt{allowed}. The
\emph{selection} request is an integer $K$. To illustrate the interface, a user asking \emph{``Find me three
configurations with F1 at least 0.85 and review budget below 0.30,
training on no more than 60\% of the data''} is mapped to
\texttt{target\_min(0.85)} with \texttt{review\_budget: range(0, 0.30)}
and \texttt{training\_fraction: range(0, 0.60)} as soft constraints; a
request for \emph{``10\% less review effort at similar quality''} is
\emph{representable} as \texttt{delta\_improve(0.0)} with the relative
constraint \texttt{review\_budget: range(0, 0.90 *
current\_budget)}. We stress the distinction between what the schema
can express and what the evaluated parser resolves: relative requests
of this form are not reliably produced by the parser
(Section~\ref{subsec:parser-eval}) and currently trigger the explicit
fallback. Deterministic resolution requires extracting the target
field, direction, and percentage, then computing the bound in code;
this is not yet implemented.

The language model is instructed to return only JavaScript Object
Notation (JSON) matching the
expected schema. The raw output is normalised to handle common
shorthand forms, validated against the schema, repaired if necessary,
and snapped to valid domains. For example, review-budget values are
snapped to the allowed grid, training fractions are clipped and
quantised to the valid range, and corruption levels are restricted to
\texttt{none}, \texttt{mild}, or \texttt{strong}. Explicit numerical
preferences detected in the user request are overlaid after parsing,
so that user-stated values take precedence over ambiguous model
interpretations.

The factual reference for generation is resolved automatically from
the optimisation log: after scoring all trials with the surrogate, the
interface selects $n$ anchor trials ($n{=}3$ by default) combining the
best-predicted trial, a median-budget trial from the upper predicted
range, and greedily diversity-maximising additions. The validated
query is applied at each anchor; per-anchor candidates are pooled,
deduplicated, ranked by predicted quality, and truncated to the
requested $K$.

The interface presents alternatives in a compact table (predicted
quality, main configuration choices, soft-control values, constraint
status), followed by a textual explanation intended to summarise the
objective, compare alternatives, identify best and runner-up, report
ties correctly, and surface shared violations once. These are design
intentions of the explanation layer; we did not audit the factual
faithfulness of the generated prose against the structured payload
(Section~\ref{subsec:limitations}). This confines the language model
to translating intent into a structured query and verbalising a
structured payload, while ranking, constraint checks, and candidates remain
grounded in the surrogate, the feature domains, and the
candidate-production method.

\section{Technical Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}
% =======================================================================

This section evaluates the proposed counterfactual-guided HPO pipeline
in the visual anomaly-detection setting. The evaluation has three
goals: assessing whether the surrogate is accurate enough for fast
local search, whether the methods return alternatives satisfying the
structured operator-facing query, and whether selected recommendations
survive confirmation by the underlying PatchCore pipeline. The pipeline is
evaluated on MVTec, the controlled benchmark, and Proditec, the
production use case, with the full three-method
comparison and $24$ confirmed candidates each, followed by the five
diagnostic analyses announced in Section~\ref{sec:introduction}
(Sections~\ref{subsec:gap-diagnosis}--\ref{subsec:parser-eval}).

\subsection{Datasets and Use Cases}
\label{subsec:datasets}

\paragraph{MVTec AD}
In our evaluation, MVTec \cite{bergmann_mvtec_2019} provides controlled
train/validation/test splits and a well-understood performance
landscape, serving as the benchmark half of the two-dataset comparison.
Our MVTec configuration log contains $9{,}976$ completed trials over
the PatchCore configuration space across four of the fifteen MVTec
categories (bottle, cable, capsule, hazelnut). This restriction is a compute-budget decision fixed in advance rather
than chosen on downstream results: every logged trial retrains
PatchCore, so four categories alone cost roughly $85$ hours of graphics
processing unit (GPU) time
(Section~\ref{subsec:compute-cost}) and all fifteen would scale that
by about $4\times$.

\paragraph{Proditec}
Unlike MVTec, Proditec data are acquired under varying production
conditions and reflect image variability, sensor noise, and imbalanced
defect frequencies. This lets
us test whether the pipeline generalises from benchmark to deployment
and whether MVTec's confirmation behaviour holds in a different data
regime. Our Proditec configuration log contains
$2{,}999$ completed trials over the same configuration space.

Both use cases share the same PatchCore configuration space
(Table~\ref{tab:configuration-space}), enabling direct comparison of
surrogate fidelity and main-model confirmation across datasets.

\subsection{Experimental Protocol}
\label{subsec:protocol}

All experiments ran on a single workstation with one NVIDIA RTX~A5500
GPU (power-capped to 70\% of its board limit), an AMD Threadripper PRO
5995WX central processing unit (CPU), and 256 gigabytes of memory. The experimental pipeline starts from
Optuna optimisation logs
collected independently over the PatchCore configuration space for
each use case. Each completed trial stores the sampled hard
hyperparameters, the soft controls, and the resulting validation F1
score. Invalid or incomplete trials are removed, categorical variables
are normalised, numerical variables are snapped to their allowed
domains, and the remaining trials are used to train the surrogate model
$\hat{f}_{\theta}$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:surrogate}) by minimising the squared
prediction error of Eq.~\ref{eq:surrogate-loss}. The original
stratified $80/20$ split is retained as a development comparison:
CatBoost uses its $20\%$ portion for early stopping, so it is not
treated as independent test evidence. The primary fidelity estimate
uses the grouped cross-validation protocol of
Section~\ref{subsec:surrogate-fidelity}.

A CatBoost regressor \cite{prokhorenkova_catboost_2018} is used as the surrogate because it provides
native handling of categorical configuration features and competitive
predictive performance on the configuration--performance dataset. On
each dataset, the surrogate is queried by three candidate-production
methods: DiCE, \textsc{LogTopK}, and ACFX, each instantiating the call of Eq.~\ref{eq:generator}. For each method
we request $K = 8$ candidates, yielding $24$ per dataset. This provides
several alternatives while keeping full confirmation feasible. When
query-satisfying candidates are insufficient, the fallback of
Section~\ref{subsec:cf-generation} retains explicitly marked
near-compliant candidates.
Candidates are filtered and ranked according to objective satisfaction,
soft-constraint satisfaction, predicted quality, and distance from the
factual configuration. A candidate satisfies its surrogate query if it meets
Eq.~\ref{eq:cf-validity} within the feasible region of
Eq.~\ref{eq:query-feasible-region}.
Since the technical evaluation has no live operator, the factual
configuration $\mathbf{x}_0$ is a logged trial chosen by the
experimenter from a pipeline-presented set containing the trials
nearest the 50th, 70th, 85th, and 95th validation-F1 percentiles plus
the best trial. The selected factual is held fixed across all three
methods. For exact reproduction,
\path{mvtec_all_methods_cfs_metadata.json} and
\path{proditec_all_methods_cfs_metadata.json} record the primary
\texttt{factual\_index}, which resolves the complete feature vector in
the released trial table; all three methods load that same payload.
Both datasets use the same methods, $K$, and free soft controls. The
recorded MVTec objective is \texttt{target\_min(0.70)}; Proditec uses
\texttt{target\_band(0.75,0.95)} (Eq.~\ref{eq:objective-types}).

Finally, every candidate is confirmed using the underlying PatchCore pipeline,
which retrains the model under the candidate configuration on the
corresponding splits and recomputes the actual validation and test F1
scores together with test area under the receiver operating
characteristic curve (AUROC). All $24$ candidates are confirmed on each
dataset ($48$ retrainings), separating fast recommendation from
main-model evidence. For the diagnostic analyses of Section~\ref{subsec:gap-diagnosis}, each
confirmed candidate is re-run once with the same nominal seeds to
capture the raw per-image anomaly scores. This is a linked diagnostic
replication of the confirmation protocol, not a seed-variability test;
the largest MVTec F1 difference between the two runs is 0.019.

\paragraph{Implementation details}
PatchCore is our own implementation over torchvision feature
extractors (\texttt{wide\_resnet50\_2}, \texttt{resnet18/34/50}, with
ImageNet weights when \texttt{pretrained} is true) rather than a
packaged library. Corruption is applied deterministically to training
(normal) images only, seeded per image path: \texttt{mild} is a
Gaussian blur of radius $0.6$; \texttt{strong} is a Gaussian blur of
radius $1.2$ followed by additive Gaussian noise ($\sigma = 8$ on the
$0$--$255$ scale, clipped); \texttt{none} leaves images unchanged.
Held-out images are divided into validation and test halves by a
label-stratified $50/50$ split. Proditec has $4{,}538$ normal
training images, validation $567$ normal / $300$ anomalous and test
$568$ / $299$, with anomaly prevalence $\approx 34.5\%$ in both;
with the same construction applied per MVTec category. HPO uses
Optuna's TPE sampler (fixed seed, \texttt{multivariate=False},
\texttt{group=False}), with $1{,}500$ start-up trials for Proditec and
$4{,}000$ for MVTec, maximising
Eq.~\ref{eq:hpo-target}, with no pruning and a fixed trial budget. The
surrogate is CatBoost ($5{,}000$ iterations with early stopping after
$200$ rounds, learning rate $0.05$, depth $8$, \texttt{l2\_leaf\_reg}
$3.0$, root mean squared error loss). DiCE uses
\texttt{method="random"} with per-feature
ranges taken from the observed log domain; ACFX fits its required
main-effects explainable boosting machine (EBM) classifier (\texttt{interpret},
\texttt{interactions=0}) to CatBoost-derived objective labels, and all
final candidates are rescored with the common CatBoost surrogate, so
the three methods share final scoring.
Software: Python 3.11.9,
PyTorch 2.10.0 (CUDA 12.8), torchvision 0.25.0, scikit-learn 1.6.1,
Optuna 4.2.0, CatBoost 1.2.8, dice-ml 0.12, acfx 0.3.6, interpret
0.7.3, transformers 5.1.0.

\subsection{Surrogate Query Satisfaction and Main-Model Confirmation}
\label{subsec:cf-validity}

Surrogate-level query satisfaction is 24/24 on both datasets: every
candidate meets its recorded objective and soft constraints under
Eq.~\ref{eq:cf-validity}. Main-model confirmation gives the
complementary result. After retraining, 21/24 Proditec candidates meet
the requested $[0.75,0.95]$ band, whereas 0/24 MVTec candidates meet
the $0.70$ minimum. The first figure verifies the structured search and
constraint layer; the second measures whether the surrogate estimate
persists in PatchCore. Section~\ref{subsec:gap-diagnosis} analyses why
these layers agree on Proditec but diverge for the primary MVTec query.

The methods differed substantially in proximity (the mixed-type
distance of Eq.~\ref{eq:mixed-distance}) and generation time,
consistently across datasets
(Table~\ref{tab:technical-evaluation-summary}): DiCE was fastest with
moderate distance, \textsc{LogTopK} fast but most distant, and ACFX two orders of
magnitude slower but closest to the factual configuration.

\subsection{Main-Model Confirmation}
\label{subsec:main-model-confirmation}

The central technical result is the contrast between successful
production confirmation and a benchmark reliability boundary. On
Proditec, the 24 recommendations average confirmed PatchCore test F1 of
$0.815$, a mean surrogate-to-model gap of only $-0.024$, and test AUROC
of $0.947$; 21 satisfy the requested target band. The same evaluation
protocol exposes different behaviour on MVTec: mean confirmed F1 is
$0.599$, the gap is $-0.190$, and pooled test AUROC is $0.497$.
Proditec therefore demonstrates that the framework can deliver useful
recommendations in the studied production setting, while MVTec shows
why aggregate surrogate performance cannot by itself authorise an
individual recommendation. Main-model confirmation remains the final
deployment gate in both settings.

Figure~\ref{fig:surrogate-vs-test} plots confirmed PatchCore F1
against surrogate F1 for every candidate on Proditec: most points lie
near the ideal-agreement line, with the tightest cluster for \textsc{LogTopK} in
the high-quality region and the largest individual gaps among ACFX
candidates.

A complementary view on Proditec compares the soft-parameter profiles

The top confirmed candidates obtain a mean confirmed PatchCore F1 of
$0.888$ versus $0.790$ for the others, using a \emph{lower} average
training fraction ($0.792$ compared with $0.832$), a lower review budget
($0.345$ compared with $0.371$), and, most strikingly, uniformly \texttt{none} or
\texttt{mild} corruption, whereas the weaker group is dominated by
\texttt{strong} corruption. A
standardised regression of confirmed F1 on the three soft controls
jointly over the 24 candidates supports this reading beyond the
post-hoc top-6 split: the strong-corruption indicator is the only
significant term (coefficient $-0.048$, $p \approx 10^{-4}$; Spearman
$\rho = -0.77$; model $R^2 = 0.67$), while training fraction and
review budget are not significant. The corruption assumption is thus
the soft control most strongly associated with confirmed quality on
Proditec, and the best configurations are not those that consume the
most training data. This is exactly the kind of
trade-off that a standard HPO ranking would hide and that the
operator-centred view is designed to surface.

defined target from threshold transfer and from cross-category
score-scale effects.

\paragraph{Within-category ranking is intact on MVTec}
Although the mean pooled test AUROC of the retrained MVTec candidates
is at chance level ($0.497$), the mean \emph{per-category} test AUROC
of the very same models is $0.827$: within each of the four categories,
the retrained candidates rank anomalies well
(Figure~\ref{fig:pooled-vs-perclass}). Two distinct effects follow
from pooling. First, because the anomaly-score scales differ across
categories, merging them destroys the \emph{global ranking}: pooled
AUROC falls to chance even though no threshold is involved, a purely
ranking-level effect ($0.827 \rightarrow 0.497$, a penalty of
$0.330$). Second, and separately, a single validation-calibrated
budget threshold applied to those incompatible scales over- or
under-flags whole categories, which damages \emph{F1}: applying the
same review budget \emph{within} each category recovers $+0.248$ mean
F1 without changing the models. The ranking and threshold effects must
therefore be interpreted separately because AUROC is
threshold-independent.

\paragraph{The discrepancy appears before validation-to-test transfer}
The surrogate's prediction target is pooled validation F1 under the
review-budget protocol (Eq.~\ref{eq:hpo-target}), so the gap must
first be split at the validation boundary. For the MVTec candidates
the retrained pooled validation F1 is $0.585 \pm 0.023$ against a mean
surrogate prediction of $0.789 \pm 0.023$: a surrogate error of
$-0.204$ on the very quantity the surrogate is trained to predict. The
subsequent validation-to-test transfer at the deployed threshold is
small ($+0.013 \pm 0.010$). The total confirmation gap of $-0.190$ is
therefore dominated by the prediction-to-rerun-validation term on the
recommended configurations, not by validation-to-test threshold transfer; on
Proditec both components are small (surrogate term
$-0.009 \pm 0.031$, transfer $-0.015 \pm 0.020$). This revises the
natural first reading of
Table~\ref{tab:technical-evaluation-summary}: a surrogate with
development $R^2 = 0.996$ and grouped $R^2 = 0.995$ can still be badly wrong on the specific
configurations a constrained query selects.

\paragraph{Pooled score-scale incompatibility is a deployment bottleneck}
The surrogate learns one pooled-validation F1 value per configuration,
but that scalar target does not preserve the category-specific score
distributions from which the pooled metric is produced. In the upper
region of the MVTec log, configurations happen to pool coherently;
query-targeted candidates generated around the selected mid-log factual
do not. The result is configuration-dependent rather than universal:
the six additional upper-quantile MVTec candidates of
Section~\ref{subsec:retrieval-comparison} retrain to pooled test AUROC
$\approx 0.98$ with gaps within $\pm 0.02$. Moreover, sweeping the
review budget over its full grid does not repair the affected candidates
(best budget improves confirmed F1 by at most $0.017$), so no choice
of $\beta$ compensates for incommensurable scales. Together with the
per-category analysis, these observations implicate the pooled
objective and calibration protocol as the operational bottleneck. They
do not establish score-scale mismatch as the sole cause of every
surrogate prediction error.

\paragraph{An oracle threshold bounds discrimination, and nothing more}
An oracle global threshold fitted on the \emph{test} labels raises
mean confirmed MVTec F1 from $0.599$ to $0.832$ (Proditec: $0.839$).
This is an upper bound on what a test-calibrated rule could extract,
not an achievable deployment result, and it does not indicate that the
surrogate predicted its target correctly.

\paragraph{Distance and configuration degeneracy are secondary}
Across all $24$ MVTec candidates, the gap correlates strongly with the
mixed-type distance of Eq.~\ref{eq:mixed-distance} (Pearson
$r=-0.91$, $p<0.001$), but this association is driven by differences
\emph{between} methods (the distant \textsc{LogTopK} candidates carry
the largest gaps); within each method the correlation is weak and not
significant (ACFX $r=-0.46$, \textsc{LogTopK} $r=-0.02$, DiCE $r=-0.54$; all
$p>0.05$). On Proditec the pooled correlation is even positive
($r=0.58$, $p=0.003$): larger distances do not produce larger gaps.
The worst-gap candidates show no configuration degeneracy on MVTec
(all $24$ use pretrained weights, none strong corruption or an extreme
coreset ratio), while Proditec's modest residual gaps concentrate in
\texttt{resnet34}/single-layer candidates under \texttt{strong}
corruption (mean gap $-0.035$ over $14$ candidates versus $-0.011$;
AUROC $0.931$ versus $0.970$).

\paragraph{Consequence: evaluate fidelity where decisions are made}
Aggregate cross-validation measures fidelity over the logged
configuration distribution, whereas a recommendation system acts on a
subset selected by its surrogate and the operator's constraints. We
refer to accuracy on that induced subset as \emph{selection-conditioned
fidelity}. The MVTec result shows why it must complement global
$R^2$: reliability is poor for the primary query-selected region but
strong for the additional upper-quantile region. Improving aggregate
surrogate fit alone would not address the separate deployment problem
created by pooling incompatible category scales. The supported design
rules are therefore to test fidelity on configurations selected by
representative queries, confirm final candidates with the underlying
model, and define and calibrate review-budget objectives per category
for heterogeneous product types.

\subsection{Sensitivity to the Factual Configuration}
\label{subsec:multi-factual}

The main method comparison (Section~\ref{subsec:cf-comparison}) uses one
factual configuration per dataset. As an auxiliary
anchor-sensitivity analysis, we repeated generation under a common
target-band query (\texttt{target\_band}
$[0.75,0.95]$, free soft controls, $K=8$) at five factual anchors drawn
from each log to span the target distribution (the trials nearest the
50th, 70th, 85th, and 95th percentiles of validation F1, plus the best
trial), covering the two fast methods, DiCE and \textsc{LogTopK} ($80$ additional surrogate-level candidates
per dataset, no additional retraining); ACFX was excluded because of
its two-orders-of-magnitude higher generation cost.

Three properties are stable across all five anchors on both datasets.
First, surrogate-level query satisfaction remains $100\%$ in every anchor--method
combination, so the post-processing and validation layer is robust to
the starting point. Second, under this auxiliary query,
\textsc{LogTopK}'s mean surrogate F1 exceeds DiCE's at every anchor,
showing stable ordering across the tested factual configurations. Third,
the methods behave as their designs suggest: \textsc{LogTopK} returns
the same top-scoring logged configurations at every anchor (constant
$\bar{F}_1$ $0.815$ on MVTec, $0.897$ on Proditec), so its distance to
the factual varies purely with the anchor's position ($1.41$--$5.65$
and $0.18$--$3.83$), whereas DiCE adapts locally, trading a lower score
near weak anchors ($\bar{F}_1$ $0.781$--$0.814$ and $0.832$--$0.893$)
for smaller distances near strong ones (down to $0.69$ and $0.09$).
This sweep is surrogate-level only; confirmation across anchors remains
future work (Section~\ref{subsec:limitations}).

\subsection{Generation versus Log Retrieval under Constrained Queries}
\label{subsec:retrieval-comparison}

We tested generation beyond the log with twelve structured queries (ten
base and two dataset-relative quantile variants) at three factual anchors
per dataset. They cover
broad, tight, absolute, quantile, one-sided, relative, combined, and
infeasible constraints. We compare DiCE with \textsc{LogTopK}, which
ranks query-satisfying logged configurations by surrogate score, and
\textsc{NearestFeasible}, which ranks them by the mixed-type distance
of Eq.~\ref{eq:mixed-distance}. For DiCE we report raw counts before
repair or domain snapping and final query satisfaction. Because
\textsc{LogTopK} is surrogate-ranked, this experiment tests generation
beyond the log, not surrogate ranking against the recorded trial F1.

Two findings summarise the suite. First, in every query cell where any
method produced query-satisfying candidates, the retrieval baselines
did as well, and DiCE
satisfied no query that retrieval could not; conversely, the absolute
tight band $[0.85, 0.90]$, the relative budget-reduction query, and the
combined tight query were infeasible on MVTec for \emph{all} methods,
generation included, because the surrogate assigns no configuration a
score in the requested region. Second, main-model confirmation of the
top candidates under the tight quantile band (two per method per
dataset, twelve additional PatchCore retrainings) shows retrieval
matching or slightly exceeding generation in confirmed quality
(Table~\ref{tab:retrieval-confirmed}). All six MVTec candidates,
drawn from the upper-quantile region of the log, retrain to pooled test
AUROC $\approx 0.98$ with gaps within $\pm 0.02$
(compare Section~\ref{subsec:gap-diagnosis}).

Across this suite, off-log generation does not increase feasible-query
coverage, and confirmation under the tight quantile band gives
comparable top-candidate quality. We read this as a
coverage-conditioned deployment result rather than a general ranking of
counterfactual methods: for the two logs studied here, retrieval is the
appropriate first-stage policy because it stays within previously
evaluated configurations without sacrificing coverage or confirmed
quality. Generation remains useful when retrieval returns too few
feasible, local, or diverse alternatives, a regime not observed in
these dense logs that should be tested under deliberately reduced
coverage. Because logged status did not guarantee confirmation under
every query, a retrieve-first policy reduces unsupported search but
does not replace main-model confirmation.

\subsection{Evaluation of the Conversational Parser}
\label{subsec:parser-eval}

The utterance-to-structured-query pipeline was previously assessed only
end-to-end through the user study. We add a direct evaluation on 40 labelled operator utterances covering
all three objective types, absolute and relative constraints,
percentage and decimal units, alternative-count extraction (including
number words), fixed-value snapping, decoy numbers, paraphrase pairs,
and deliberately infeasible or out-of-domain requests.
Two front-ends are scored against field-level gold annotations: the
one deployed during the user study, and the released version, which
adds a factual-aware prompt with worked examples, coercion of
one-sided constraint phrasings, and one error-aware retry, keeping
schema, validator, repair, and fallback layers identical. Scoring is exact at
the field level after canonical snapping, with a documented
semantic-equivalence variant that credits operationally identical
encodings; fallback outputs receive no credit under either scoring.

Table~\ref{tab:parser-eval} summarises the results. The study-time
front-end produced schema-valid JSON on the first attempt for 12.5\% of
utterances, fell back for 87.5\%, and achieved 5\% exact full-query
accuracy. The revised front-end raises first-attempt acceptance to
72.5\%, cuts fallback to 22.5\%, and reaches 60\% exact (62.5\%
semantic) accuracy; exact accuracy among accepted JSON outputs is 77\%.
Greedy decoding makes repeated parses identical. Both front-ends score
0\% on relative constraints such as ``10\% less review effort'': the
three-billion-parameter model cannot reliably perform arithmetic against the current
configuration, so these requests trigger the explicit fallback. This
matches the user study, where condition-B participants needed most
attempts on baseline-relative Task~3 (6.3, compared with 1.6 and 3.5 on
absolute Tasks~1 and~2) because iteration, anchoring, and overlay
resolved relative requests. Although the released front-end handles
absolute requests substantially better, 40\% of labelled utterances
are still not parsed exactly. The parser should therefore extract the
relative field, direction, and quantity, then resolve the arithmetic
deterministically before candidate production.

\section{Evaluation with Human Participants}
\label{sec:user-study}
% =======================================================================

The proposed framework is intended to support human decision-making
during configuration selection, not only to generate query-satisfying
counterfactual alternatives. We therefore conducted a user evaluation
to examine whether participants could select machine-learning
configurations under quality and deployment-oriented constraints, and
whether the conversational interface supported comparison of
alternatives and understanding of configuration trade-offs. The study
was run with computer-science master's students rather than industrial
operators; it therefore tests whether the interface makes
constraint-based configuration selection tractable for
technically literate non-specialists, which is a necessary but not
sufficient condition for deployment with production operators
(Section~\ref{subsec:limitations}).

\subsection{Study Design}
\label{subsec:study-design}

The study used a between-subjects design with two conditions: (A) an
application programming interface (API) baseline, using the pipeline
through its Python API in a Jupyter
notebook with criteria in code, and (B) the conversational interface,
with natural-language criteria and textual explanations alongside
tabular results. Both conditions used the same pipeline: anchors were
selected from the log (Section~\ref{subsec:interface}), candidates were
generated under the constraints, scored by the surrogate, and merged
into a presented top-$K$. The conditions differed only in how criteria
were expressed and results presented.

Under the university requirements applicable to this minimal-risk,
course-based software-usability evaluation, no separate
ethics-committee review was required. The responsible academic
supervisor and the course coordinator authorised the study.
Participation was voluntary and
unpaid, did not affect course assessment, and participants could withdraw
at any time. All participants received study information and provided
written informed consent. Each session began
by explaining that the tool, not the participant, was evaluated. Participants then
completed three configuration-selection tasks. In each task, they
selected one final configuration and wrote a short justification
explaining their choice. After completing the tasks, participants
answered a short post-task questionnaire about their experience with
the assigned system.

Tasks~1 and~2 evaluated selection under \emph{absolute} constraints on
predicted F1 score, training fraction, review budget, and corruption
level. Task~3 evaluated selection under constraints defined
\emph{relative} to a base configuration: participants had to select an
alternative that preserved predicted F1 score while reducing review
budget by at least 5\%.
Table~\ref{tab:user-study-tasks} summarises the task requirements.

\subsection{Participants and Measures}
\label{subsec:participants-measures}

Twenty-two computer-science master's students participated (one
condition-A participant's duplicated session was removed from the logs,
leaving 22 unique participants across 10 API and 12
conversational-interface sessions). All were enrolled in a
computer-science master's programme and had completed coursework in
machine learning, so they were familiar with model training and
hyperparameters but were not industrial inspection operators;
Section~\ref{subsec:limitations} discusses what this proxy population
does and does not license. Participants were recruited during
laboratory sessions; none was an author or involved in developing the
system. Participants were randomly assigned to conditions. Beyond programme enrolment and
prior machine-learning coursework, no demographic data were collected,
which we note as a limitation (Section~\ref{subsec:limitations}).

A task answer was
counted as \emph{usable} if the session log contained an explicitly
marked final configuration selection for that task. The cleaned
task-level dataset contained 65 usable final answers: 30 from
condition A and 35 from condition B. Condition A contained 10 usable
answers for each task; condition B contained 12 for Tasks~1 and~2 and
11 for Task~3, because one participant completed the session without
recording a final Task~3 selection.

After removing one response without a valid identifier and one
duplicate (later submission kept), 20 valid questionnaire responses
remained (9 condition A, 11 condition B); the shortfall from 22
reflects voluntary completion. The questionnaire contained five Likert-scale items
(1--5, higher is more positive): ease of identifying valid F1
configurations, checking soft constraints, understanding trade-offs,
comparing options, and using the provided tool. Together with the
overall small sample size, the mild imbalance (9 compared with 11) means that
Likert comparisons should be treated as descriptive indicators rather
than reliable effect estimates.

For each final answer, we recorded requirement satisfaction, the
number and type of violated constraints, the number of attempts, and
the written justification; for Task~3, additionally the base
configuration, selected alternative, quality change, and review-budget
reduction. Attempt counts are descriptive because interaction steps
were logged differently across environments.

differences rather than as reliable effect estimates.

\subsection{Qualitative Analysis of Participant Justifications}
\label{subsec:justification-analysis}

To complement the quantitative results we analysed the short written
justifications qualitatively, using a large language model as an
automated judge \cite{zheng_judging_2023} to reduce confirmation bias.
Anonymised justifications with condition, task, and
selected-configuration metadata were submitted under a structured
prompt covering attribute references, trade-off reasoning, condition
differences, Task-3 what-if reasoning, and over-reliance on predicted
F1, requiring descriptive analysis without overclaiming or identifying
participants. The authors reviewed the summary for factual consistency
with the raw comments; the model may still miss subtle patterns, and
the output reflects the prompt's framing.

\paragraph{Overall pattern}
Across conditions and tasks, participants grounded choices in a small
set of observable attributes: predicted F1, review budget, and, less
often, training fraction and corruption level. They provided brief,
outcome-oriented justifications (most often highest F1 or lowest
budget) and a minority engaging in deliberative trade-off reasoning;
justification length was marginally higher in condition B.

\paragraph{Condition differences and design implications}
The two conditions produced similar strategies with different framing:
condition-A participants anchored on F1 maximisation, while
condition-B participants more often referenced named options and the
interface's ``balanced compromise'' framing, occasionally prioritising
corruption level over F1. Three tentative design implications follow:
secondary metrics (budget reduction, corruption robustness) should be
scaffolded as first-class criteria; baseline-relative constraints need
salient, continuously updated satisfaction feedback; and evaluative
option labels should be used with care, since they may be accepted
uncritically.

\paragraph{Task~3 and baseline-relative reasoning}
Task~3 elicited the most varied justifications: the \emph{relative}
budget constraint led to at least two invalid selections believed to
be valid, and several participants in both conditions optimised
absolute F1 gain or the absolute budget value rather than the
percentage reduction, suggesting that relative framing is cognitively
less accessible than absolute comparisons.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% =======================================================================

\subsection{Interpreting the Two-Dataset Technical Results}
\label{subsec:discussion-technical}

The paired evaluation establishes a useful production result and the
condition needed to trust it. On Proditec, 21/24 recommendations meet
their target after retraining, with mean confirmed F1 of $0.815$, gap
of $-0.024$, and AUROC of $0.947$. On MVTec, all 24 recommendations
satisfy the surrogate query but the underlying model does not confirm
the target. The $-0.204$ prediction-to-rerun-validation term and
$+0.013$ validation-to-test term localise the discrepancy to the
validation side rather than to test transfer. At the same time,
per-category AUROC of $0.827$, pooled AUROC of $0.497$, and a $+0.248$
F1 gain from category-wise thresholds show that the pooled objective is
unsuitable for the heterogeneous score scales in this deployment.
Upper-quantile gaps within $\pm 0.02$ further show that reliability is
selection-conditioned rather than uniformly poor. The different
primary objectives prevent a controlled cross-dataset comparison, but
together the experiments support a general evaluation rule: global
surrogate fidelity must be complemented by confirmation on the region
induced by representative operator queries.

\subsection{Implications for Operator-Centred HPO}
\label{subsec:discussion-operator}

The user evaluation concerns the complete study-time workflow.
Technically literate non-specialists produced high task validity in
both conditions and reported greater ease of comparing options with the
conversational interface. Because the study-time parser achieved only
5\% exact full-query accuracy, these task results cannot be attributed
to one-shot parsing; they reflect the combined interaction, including
repair, anchoring, presentation, fallback, and iteration. The present
study does not isolate the contribution of each layer, but it shows why
parser accuracy and workflow task success must be evaluated separately.
Baseline-relative requirements were hardest in both conditions, and
quality maximisation sometimes displaced the requested budget
reduction. The labelled parser test identifies a direct remedy: treat
relative constraints as first-class objects, extract their field,
direction, and quantity, and resolve the arithmetic deterministically.
The revised parser and the target population of production operators
are the next evaluation setting.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{subsec:limitations}

\paragraph{Scope of datasets and task}
The evaluation covers one benchmark (four MVTec categories) and one
production use case on a single architecture (PatchCore) with a fixed
configuration space. The score-scale pathology is observed in the
pooled four-category MVTec setting and not in Proditec; two datasets do
not establish that this difference generalises to all multi-category
and single-product deployments.

\paragraph{Confirmation coverage}
Confirmation covers all $24$ main candidates per dataset plus the $12$
retrieval-comparison candidates, but the multi-anchor sweep is
surrogate-level only and per-candidate variability rests on single
confirmations with one seeded re-run each.

\paragraph{Language-model parsing reliability}
The released front-end parses 60\% of labelled utterances exactly
(77\% among accepted JSON outputs) and falls back on 22.5\%. Relative
constraints fail entirely at the parser level. This is an addressable
architecture limitation rather than a reason to delegate arithmetic to
a larger language model: the parser can extract the quantity and
reference field, after which deterministic code computes the bound.
The user study used the earlier front-end (5\% exact accuracy), so its
results apply to the complete workflow, including repair, anchoring,
overlay, fallback, and iteration, rather than to one-shot parsing.

\paragraph{User-study population}
The participants were computer-science master's students, not
industrial inspection operators. They are a proxy for reasoning about
model quality, training data, and thresholds without being specialists
in the deployed pipeline. Recruiting
comparable numbers of production operators was not feasible for this
study. They nonetheless differ from the target users in ways that plausibly
matter: operators carry domain priors about defects and inspection
cost, face real consequences for a wrong configuration, and may weigh
review effort differently. The study therefore supports usability
claims for technically literate non-specialists and motivates, rather
than substitutes for, a planned operator-population study.

\paragraph{User-study scale and design}
The user study is exploratory: $22$ participants across two conditions,
descriptive statistics only, condition-specific logging of attempt
counts, and voluntary questionnaire completion with a mild response
imbalance. Demographic attributes beyond programme enrolment and prior
machine-learning coursework were not collected, so their influence
cannot be analysed. The results should be read as design evidence, not
as confirmatory effect estimates.

\paragraph{No surrogate support indicator}
The MVTec results make the absence of a per-candidate reliability signal
a substantive gap. The interface already computes mixed-type distance
to the factual and logged status, so it could flag weakly supported
candidates and recommend confirmation.
Logged status alone is insufficient because the primary MVTec
\textsc{LogTopK} candidates also show large gaps; a useful signal
must combine support, local error, and target stability. Such a signal
is not part of the evaluated system.

\paragraph{Explanation faithfulness unevaluated}
The parser is evaluated, but the generated textual explanations are
not. Because they are produced from a structured payload, a
mechanically checkable faithfulness suite (agreement with the
candidate table, correct constraint status, tie preservation, no
unsupported trade-offs) is feasible and is planned; until then, the
explanations should be regarded as unverified presentation rather
than validated evidence.

\paragraph{Energy measurement}
No component or end-to-end power telemetry was collected. Runtime is
reported in Section~\ref{subsec:compute-cost}, but no energy or CO$_2$
saving is claimed.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% =======================================================================

This paper presented CF-XHPO, a framework that turns HPO logs into
operator-centred what-if guidance for industrial visual anomaly
detection. It combines surrogate modelling, constrained retrieval,
counterfactual generation, deterministic query validation, and a
conversational interface. The evaluation confirms 60 selected
configurations with PatchCore and demonstrates practical value on the
Proditec production dataset: 21/24 primary recommendations meet their
target after retraining, with mean confirmed F1 of $0.815$, mean gap of
$-0.024$, and test AUROC of $0.947$.

The evaluation also establishes how such guidance should be validated.
All 48 primary recommendations satisfy their surrogate-level queries,
but the underlying model confirms 21/24 on Proditec and 0/24 for the
primary MVTec query. MVTec therefore exposes selection-conditioned
surrogate error that global $R^2$ does not reveal. The score-level
analysis localises the discrepancy to the validation side and shows a
separate deployment problem: incompatible category score scales reduce
pooled AUROC from $0.827$ per category to $0.497$ and make one pooled
review-budget threshold unsuitable. The resulting policy is concrete:
evaluate fidelity on query-selected regions, calibrate heterogeneous
categories separately, and confirm final recommendations with the
underlying model.

The supporting analyses translate these results into system-design
choices. Constrained retrieval matches off-log generation on the tested
dense logs, motivating a retrieve-first policy with generation reserved
for insufficient coverage, proximity, or diversity. Complete
interaction overhead is substantial but amortises across an
eight-candidate screen, producing a clear wall-clock benefit on
Proditec and on the faster MVTec routes under a sequential-retraining
comparator. In the user study, both workflows support high task
validity, while conversational participants report greater ease of
option comparison. The parser evaluation separates this workflow-level
result from one-shot parsing and identifies deterministic resolution of
relative constraints as the immediate interface improvement. Together,
these findings position CF-XHPO not as an automatic replacement for
model evaluation, but as an empirically validated screening and
decision-support layer over trials that an HPO process has already
produced.

Future work will define and calibrate the review-budget objective per
category, resolve relative constraints deterministically before
generation, surface a support indicator distinguishing logged from
generated candidates, evaluate the factual faithfulness of the
generated explanations, repeat the user evaluation with the released
parser and with production operators, and extend confirmation across
anchors, seeds, and further industrial use cases.
